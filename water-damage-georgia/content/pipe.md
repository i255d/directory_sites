# Pipes by decade — notes and pieces for city pages

Kept out of `scrape.md`, which is competitor text. Use pieces of this on different city pages, one angle per city, so no two FAQs say the same thing.

General U.S. eras, not a specific house. Builders mixed materials and many houses have been repiped. Never say a named neighborhood has a certain pipe. Say "may" and send people to a plumber. Not yet checked against a Georgia source; confirm before leaning on a date.

## Supply pipes by build decade

| Built | What you may find | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Before 1960 | Galvanized steel, some lead | Galvanized rusts from the inside, so pressure drops. Lead is rare. |
| 1960s to 1980s | Mostly copper | Long-lived. Lead solder was banned in 1986. |
| Late 1970s to mid-1990s | Copper, **polybutylene (PB)**, or CPVC | PB is gray plastic with a reputation for failing. Largely stopped by the mid-1990s. |
| 1980s to now | **CPVC** (cream or yellow plastic) | Common in many builds. Older CPVC can get brittle. |
| Mid-1990s to now | **PEX** (flexible, red, blue, or white) | Standard in most new houses. Rarely fails. Leaks come from fixtures and appliance lines. |

**Drains:** older houses have cast iron, which can crack or corrode. Newer ones have PVC or ABS plastic.

## How to look (safe for any page)

- Look where pipes leave the water heater, under a sink, or where the main line comes into the house.
- Gray plastic, sometimes marked PB2110: may be polybutylene.
- Cream or yellow plastic: CPVC.
- Copper-colored metal: copper.
- Flexible red, blue, or white tubing: PEX.
- Not sure? Take a photo and ask a plumber. Do not assume either way.

## Pieces to use

**Short version (any page).** Houses built in different decades use different pipes. Copper is the long-lasting one. Gray plastic pipe (polybutylene) was used in some houses until the mid-1990s and is known to fail. Newer houses use cream CPVC or flexible PEX. You can't tell which you have from the neighborhood, so look at the pipes near the water heater, or ask a plumber.

**Snellville FAQ (1980s house).** Find the main shut-off, and show everyone in the house where it is. Look at the water heater for rust, drips, or a puddle, and find out how old it is. Look at the washer hoses and the line to the refrigerator. Look at the pipes near the water heater. If they are gray plastic, that may be polybutylene. You cannot tell from the neighborhood, so ask a plumber about your house.

**Lawrenceville FAQ (late 1980s).** Look at the pipes where they come out of the wall near the water heater. If they are gray plastic, that may be polybutylene, which was used in many houses until the mid-1990s and is known to fail. Ask a plumber about your house. Do not assume it either way.

**Hamilton Mill FAQ (1990s house).** *Do the pipes in a house from the late 1990s wear out?* Usually the pipes themselves are not the problem. The failures are at the water heater, the connections under sinks and behind toilets, and the AC drain line. A house this age is also past the years when gray plastic pipe was used, so a leak is more likely to come from something connected to the pipe than from the pipe.

**Dacula FAQ addition (around 2000).** After "What usually breaks?": "The pipes in the walls are usually the least of it. The water heater, the refrigerator line, and the washer hose go first."

**Lilburn, if built (older house).** Houses from the 1970s may have copper that has lasted, and some from the late 1970s to 1990s may have gray plastic pipe. The oldest may have cast iron drain lines, which can crack. Check the house, and ask a plumber.

## Do not say

- That any neighborhood has PB, CPVC, or any other pipe.
- That PB "will fail." Say "known to fail."
- That PEX or copper "never" leaks.
- Anything about lead, health, or testing the water. This is a request site, not a plumber.
- Anything about repiping prices or that we do plumbing. The crew dries the house. A plumber fixes the pipe.
