# Rank and Rent: Building a Local Lead Site That Reaches the Top 3

Working outline. Assumes the reader has already chosen a niche and a city. The book starts at choosing the URL.

Every chapter follows the same pattern: **What it is, Why it matters, What to do, What to avoid.** Each term is explained the first time it appears.

---

## Before you start: the words used in this book

A one-page primer.

- **How Google shows local results.** A search like "plumber near me" produces two lists: the **map pack** (three businesses on a map) and the **organic results** (the regular blue links). A site can compete in both, but they work differently.
- **What "ranking" means.** Your position in those lists. "Top 3" means being one of the first three results.
- **What "rank and rent" means.** You build a site that ranks well for local searches from people who have a problem they will pay to fix. You then rent the website itself to a local business for a monthly fee. What the renter gets for that fee is every visitor who asks for more information (a phone call or a form), along with their contact details. The bet is that some percentage of those people will become paying customers.
- **What a "lead" is.** A person who asked for information and left their contact details. The site produces leads. The renter turns some of them into customers.
- **How each chapter is organized.** What it is, why it matters, what to do, what to avoid.

## Part 1: Foundation

### Ch. 1: Choosing the URL
- **What it is:** A **domain name** is the site's address (`example.com`). The **TLD** is the ending (`.com`, `.net`). An **exact-match domain** is a name that is the search phrase, like `austinroofrepair.com`.
- **Why it matters:** It's the first thing a customer sees. It helps people trust the site. When other sites link to you with just the address, the link text contains your keywords for free (this link text is called **anchor text**).
- **What to do:**
  - Pick a name that says what you do and where.
  - Keep it short and easy to spell and say aloud.
  - Check that the name isn't someone's trademark.
  - Buy the domain, point it to a host, and set up **DNS** (the settings that connect the name to your site).
- **What to avoid:**
  - Expecting the name to do the ranking. Google stopped rewarding exact-match domains in 2012.
  - Hyphens, odd spellings, and very long names.
  - A weak site on an exact-match name, which looks like spam.

### Ch. 2: Planning your pages (site structure)
- **What it is:** Your **site structure** is the list of pages you'll have and how they link to each other. Think of it as a table of contents. A **hierarchy** is a clear order: home page, then service pages, then city pages. An **internal link** is a link from one of your pages to another.
- **Why it matters:** Google learns what your site is about by following your internal links. A clear structure helps Google, and it helps a visitor find the right page fast.
- **What to do:**
  - Make one page per service and one page per city you want to rank in.
  - Link from the home page to every main page, and link nearby cities to each other.
  - Give each page its own focus: one main search phrase per page.
  - Put that phrase in the **title tag** (the headline shown in Google's results), the **H1** (the large headline on the page) and the first paragraph. Also put it in the **slug** (the last part of the web address, like `/roof-repair`), though that matters least.
- **What to avoid:**
  - Pages made for every variation of a phrase ("repair," "fix," "fixing" and so on). Choose one phrase per page.
  - Pages no other page links to.
- **How many pages:** Sources range from about 8-15 pages for one city to hundreds for a state, so present it as a range, not a rule.

### Ch. 3: Staying out of Google's spam rules
- **What it is:** Google has written rules against pages made only to climb the rankings. Two matter here.
  - **Doorway pages:** nearly identical pages made to catch different searches, which only push visitors on to somewhere else. A common example is the same page repeated for 30 cities with only the name changed.
  - **Scaled content:** lots of pages made mainly to manipulate rankings, not to help people. It counts even if AI wrote them.
- **Why it matters:** Pages that break these rules may never show up in Google, or may be removed. A site full of them can lose all its traffic.
- **What to do:**
  - Make each city page the actual destination. A visitor should find the phone number, the details and the answers right there.
  - Include things that are true only of that city.
  - Skip a city if you have nothing real to say about it.
- **What to avoid:**
  - Swapping the city name and nothing else.
  - A block of text that just lists towns.
  - Pages that exist only to send people to a form.
- **How to check:** Google gives free tools for this. **Search Console** shows which pages Google has added to its listings. Two messages to know:
  - **"Crawled - currently not indexed":** Google looked at the page and chose not to list it.
  - **"Duplicate without user-selected canonical":** Google decided two of your pages are the same and picked one itself. (A **canonical** is a tag telling Google which version of a page is the main one.)
  - If many pages show these messages, they're probably too similar.

## Part 2: Content

### Ch. 4: Researching a page before you write it
- **What it is:** Collecting facts before writing. That means looking at what the top-ranking pages say, what customers ask, and what is true about the place.
- **Why it matters:** Pages with real, specific information are the ones Google lists. Generic ones are the ones it skips.
- **What to do:**
  - Read the top three or four results for your phrase and note what they cover and what they leave out.
  - Collect real questions from Google's **"People also ask"** box.
  - Write down a source for every fact.
- **What to avoid:** Guessing, and copying competitors' wording.

### Ch. 5: Writing service and city pages
- **What it is:** The actual page copy: headline, opening, services, how the work goes, answers to common questions, and a call to action (a clear prompt to call or fill in the form).
- **Why it matters:** Content is the biggest ranking factor you control. For regular organic results, Whitespark's 2026 survey puts page content first.
- **What to do:**
  - Open with the service and the city.
  - Add local details and a short FAQ in question-and-answer form, which also helps AI answer tools quote you.
  - Show real experience (Google calls this **E-E-A-T**: experience, expertise, authority and trust).
  - Use AI for a first draft if you like, then check the facts and make it specific.
- **What to avoid:**
  - Claims you can't back up, such as response times, licences or years in business that belong to someone else.
  - Health or coverage promises.

### Ch. 6: Trust pages and honest disclosure
- **What it is:** The pages that show who's behind the site: contact, privacy policy, about, and a plain statement of how the site works.
- **Why it matters:** Visitors trust a site more when it explains itself. Hiding that the site is a referral service can hurt your reputation and cause legal trouble.
- **What to do:** Say clearly that you connect people with local companies.
- **What to avoid:** Pretending to be the company doing the work.

## Part 3: Technical and on-page SEO

*SEO (search engine optimization) means making a site easy for Google to understand and list.*

### Ch. 7: On-page basics
- **What it is:** The pieces of a page that Google reads first: title tag, **meta description** (the short summary under the title in results), headings, and **alt text** (a text label for each image).
- **Why it matters:** They tell Google what the page is about, and they decide whether people click your result.
- **What to do:** Write a clear, appealing title that starts with the main phrase. Write a summary that makes someone want to click.
- **What to avoid:** Stuffing in keywords. Write for the reader.

### Ch. 8: Structured data (schema)
- **What it is:** Hidden code that spells out facts for Google, such as business type, services and FAQ answers.
- **Why it matters:** It can help Google understand the page and show extra details in results.
- **What to do:** Add the types that fit your pages (business, service, FAQ).
- **What to avoid:** Marking up things that aren't true, such as a business address you don't have.

### Ch. 9: Speed, mobile and getting listed
- **What it is:** Most local searches happen on phones. **Core Web Vitals** are Google's speed and stability measurements. A **sitemap** is a list of your pages for Google to read. **Indexing** means Google adding a page to its listings.
- **Why it matters:** A slow or broken page loses visitors and rankings. A page that isn't indexed can't show up at all.
- **What to do:** Test on a phone. Submit your sitemap in Search Console.
- **What to avoid:** Huge images, pop-ups that cover the page, and pages blocked from Google by mistake.

### Ch. 10: Turning visitors into calls
- **What it is:** Design that gets people to call or fill in the form.
- **Why it matters:** Rankings are worth nothing if visitors don't reach out. Every call or form is a lead, and leads are what make the site worth renting.
- **What to do:** Put a tap-to-call button on every page and keep forms short.
- **What to avoid:** Long forms and hidden phone numbers.

## Part 4: Off-page and local signals

*These are things outside your own site that affect rankings.*

### Ch. 11: Google Business Profile
- **What it is:** A free Google listing for a business that appears on Google Maps and in the map pack.
- **Why it matters:** It's the biggest factor for the map pack (32% in Whitespark's 2026 survey).
- **What you need to know:** Google's rules exclude "lead generation agents or companies," and they require a real, staffed location. Virtual offices and PO boxes are not allowed. So your site usually can't have its own profile. The renter's profile does the map work.
- **What to do:** Plan for the site to win organic results and the renter's profile to cover the map.
- **What to avoid:** Fake addresses and fake locations. They can get a profile suspended.

### Ch. 12: Reviews
- **What it is:** Ratings and comments from customers on Google.
- **Why it matters:** They're the second-biggest map-pack factor (20%) and build trust.
- **What to do:** Help the renter ask happy customers for reviews.
- **What to avoid:** Fake reviews.

### Ch. 13: Citations
- **What it is:** A mention of a business's name, address and phone (**NAP**) on another website, like a directory.
- **Why it matters:** Matching details across the web help Google trust a business is real. They matter more for AI answers than for the map pack.
- **What to do:** Keep the details identical everywhere.
- **What to avoid:** Mismatched details and junk directories.

### Ch. 14: Backlinks
- **What it is:** A link to your site from another site.
- **Why it matters:** Google treats links as votes of trust. They matter more for organic results (24% in the survey) than the map pack (8%).
- **What to do:** Get links from local places such as chambers of commerce, sponsorships and community sites. Check how many different sites link to your competitors (**referring domains**).
- **What to avoid:** Bought links and link schemes.

## Part 5: Tracking and renting

### Ch. 15: Tracking calls and leads
- **What it is:** A **tracking number** is a phone number that counts the calls it gets, so you can prove the site works.
- **Why it matters:** A tenant pays rent for the leads the site produces. Counting them gives you proof of what the site delivers.
- **What to do:** Set up call tracking and form tracking, then keep a record of lead counts.
- **What to avoid:** Counting spam calls as leads.

### Ch. 16: Measuring your rankings
- **What it is:** Checking where you appear and whether it's improving.
- **Why it matters:** It shows what's working. Sources claim 3-6 months to rank in weak markets, but that is an estimate, not a promise.
- **What to do:** Use Search Console and a rank tracker. Record leads (calls and forms) per month.
- **What to avoid:** Judging after a few weeks.

### Ch. 17: Renting the site
- **What it is:** Renting the website itself to a local business for a monthly fee. The renter receives the contact details of everyone who asks for information through the site. You keep ownership of the site.
- **Why it matters:** It's how the site earns money. Some percentage of the leads will become paying customers, which is what makes the rent worth paying.
- **What to do:** Write a clear agreement covering the rent, how leads are delivered, who owns the site, and what happens at the end.
- **What to avoid:** Handing over the phone number or the site without a contract. Promising how many leads become customers, since that depends on the renter.

### Ch. 18: Keeping it going
- **What it is:** Regular upkeep.
- **Why it matters:** Rankings slip when a site goes stale, and Google's rules change.
- **What to do:** Run a monthly check and refresh old pages.

## Where the research is weaker

- **Percentages.** Ranking-factor numbers are from a survey of 47 experts, not from Google. A commonly shared set of numbers is [misattributed](https://seomadman.com/research/local-seo-ranking-factors-verifiable). Use [Whitespark's report](https://whitespark.ca/local-search-ranking-factors/).
- **Operator advice.** Most rank-and-rent guides come from people selling courses, so treat their results and timelines as unproven.
- **Policy risk.** Google's rules on [doorway pages](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) and [Business Profiles](https://support.google.com/business/answer/13762416?hl=en) are the model's weak points, and the book should say so.
